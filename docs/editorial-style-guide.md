# Editorial Style Guide

Version: **1.0.0** (Plan 08)

This guide applies to user-facing category names, concept labels, definitions/descriptions, adaptive-detail labels, and safety/negotiation copy.

## Voice and purpose

Use neutral, plain language. Define what an item generally means without implying that the user should want it, that it is unusual, or that interest in it predicts identity, morality, or behavior. Prefer concrete distinctions over euphemism, but avoid procedural instruction—especially for physical, medical, consent-complex, privacy, digital-security, reputational, or financial risk domains.

## Labels

- Start user-facing labels with a capital letter unless an established stylization genuinely requires otherwise.
- Prefer a short noun phrase over a question or sentence.
- Use singular/plural according to the thing being rated: a general practice can be singular ("Rope bondage"), while a device family may naturally be plural ("Vibrators").
- Use ` / ` with spaces when two parallel plain-language terms are intentionally paired (for example, "Chest / breast-focused nonpenetrative sex").
- Preserve established compact forms where spacing would reduce clarity, including **D/s** and established compound labels such as **master/slave-style**.
- Parentheses should clarify a genuine distinction, not carry essential consent information that belongs in the main label/definition.
- Avoid redundant "consensual" in ordinary adult activities because informed adult consent is a global premise. Keep explicit consent language where it changes the meaning, such as CNC, observation/recording, coercion fantasy, or scenarios that could otherwise imply nonconsenting participants.

## Adult-only wording

Age-coded, caregiver/little, sleep/unconsciousness, authority, or similar sensitive roleplay must make adult participation explicit in the label or immediately visible definition. Never use wording that could read as an invitation to involve actual minors or nonconsenting people.

## Definitions and descriptions

A useful description should do at least one of the following, and usually two:

1. explain what the concept means in plain language;
2. distinguish it from a nearby concept;
3. clarify role/direction/context where that distinction matters;
4. note that fantasy appeal and real-world desire can differ when the concept is primarily fantastical.

Descriptions should normally be one or two concise sentences. The retired pattern `Neutral adult self-assessment item covering …` must not be used for user-facing concepts.

## Specialist terminology

Define specialist terms on first presentation in the catalog. Examples include **compersion, predicament bondage, dollification, brat-taming, ruined orgasm, primal play, protocol, service submission, objectification, consensual non-consent (CNC), mummification, edging, chastity,** and **urethral sounding**. Definitions should be descriptive, not instructional.

## Concept vs detail

A label becomes a first-class concept when a user could reasonably like it and dislike a nearby option and that distinction would matter for self-understanding or partner communication. Repetitive permutations that only specify form, material, body location, control mode, or fantasy subtype should use adaptive details when the underlying preference is shared.

## Safety and risk wording

Risk metadata describes concern domains, not moral severity. Use high-level prompts about negotiation, stopping mechanisms, privacy, knowledge, care, or financial boundaries. Do not include step-by-step instructions for dangerous activities.
